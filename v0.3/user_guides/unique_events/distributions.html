2.4. Parametric distributions#

A parametric lifetime distribution assumes a fixed functional shape (Weibull, Gamma, Gompertz, …) for the survival function, and estimates its parameters from data. Fitting one is a single call, accounting for censoring and truncation as described in Censoring and truncation:

>>> from assetlife.datasets import load_circuit_breaker
>>> from assetlife.lifetime_models import Weibull
>>> dataset = load_circuit_breaker()
>>> weibull = Weibull().fit(dataset["time"], event=dataset["event"], entry=dataset["entry"])
>>> weibull.get_params()
array([3.7267452 , 0.01232326])

The two fitted values are the Weibull shape and rate.

2.4.1. Comparing candidate shapes#

Nothing in the data tells you which shape to assume, so fit several and look at them. The Kaplan-Meier estimator (see Non-parametric lifetime models) is the natural reference here: it makes no shape assumption, so a parametric curve that strays far from it is a poor description of the data.

(Source code, svg)

All four shapes agree while the fleet is young and only separate past about 55 years: Gompertz and Weibull bend down faster, Gamma and LogLogistic keep a heavier tail. Gompertz stays closest to the Kaplan-Meier function over the observed range.

Curves can look equally plausible, and a distribution with more parameters can always be made to hug the data more closely. The comparison has to be made on the likelihood and the number of parameters.

2.4.2. Maximum likelihood#

Fitting maximizes the likelihood of the observed data under the chosen shape. Each row contributes a factor that depends on what was actually observed.

\(\theta\) denotes the parameters, \(f\) the probability density, \(S\) the survival function, \(F\) the cumulative distribution function and \(H\) the cumulative hazard.

With \(\mathcal{D}\) the set of observed failures, \(\mathcal{C}\) the right-censored ones, \(\mathcal{I}\) the interval-censored ones and \(a_i\) the entry age of asset \(i\), fit minimizes:

\[-\log L(\theta) = - \sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}} \log f(t_i; \theta) + \sum_{i \in \mathcal{C}} H(t_i; \theta) - \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} \log \left[ F(b_i; \theta) - F(a_i; \theta) \right] - \sum_i H(a_i; \theta)\]

The four contributions to the likelihood can be interpreted as follows:

What is known about the asset

Contribution to \(-\log L(\theta)\)

it failed at \(t\) (complete observation)

\(-\log f(t;\theta)\)

it was still alive at \(t\) (right censoring)

\(H(t;\theta)\)

it failed somewhere in \([a, b]\) (interval censoring)

\(-\log \left[ F(b;\theta) - F(a;\theta) \right]\)

it was already \(a\) units old when observation began (left truncation)

\(-H(a;\theta)\)

The first three are mutually exclusive: a row is either complete, right-censored or interval-censored. The fourth one adds to whichever of them applies, on every row that entered observation after birth. Right censoring is the interval case with \(b = +\infty\) and left censoring the interval case with \(a = 0\), which is exactly how they are encoded in AssetLife (see Censoring and truncation).

2.4.3. Information criteria#

The log-likelihood alone can’t arbitrate between shapes with different numbers of parameters, since adding parameters can only improve the fit. Information criteria penalize that: with \(k\) parameters, \(n\) observations and \(\hat{\ell}\) the maximized log-likelihood,

\[\textrm{AIC} = 2k - 2\hat{\ell} \qquad \textrm{AICc} = \textrm{AIC} + \frac{2k(k+1)}{n - k - 1} \qquad \textrm{BIC} = k \log n - 2\hat{\ell}\]

AICc is the small-sample correction to AIC, and BIC penalizes parameters more heavily as the sample grows. All three are computed on fitting_results, and in every case the lower the better:

>>> print(weibull.fitting_results)
fitted params : [3.72675, 0.0123233]
AIC           : 2493.72
AICc          : 2493.72
BIC           : 2506.41

>>> from assetlife.lifetime_models import Gamma, Gompertz
>>> gamma = Gamma().fit(dataset["time"], event=dataset["event"], entry=dataset["entry"])
>>> print(gamma.fitting_results)
fitted params : [5.57387, 0.0644293]
AIC           : 2503.5
AICc          : 2503.5
BIC           : 2516.19

>>> gompertz = Gompertz().fit(dataset["time"], event=dataset["event"], entry=dataset["entry"])
>>> print(gompertz.fitting_results)
fitted params : [0.00390781, 0.0757955]
AIC           : 2485.57
AICc          : 2485.57
BIC           : 2498.25

All three shapes have two parameters here, so the penalty term is identical and the ranking is the ranking of the likelihoods: Gompertz first, then Weibull, then Gamma. This confirms what the plot suggested.

Covariates are the next step: see Parametric regressions for models where lifetime depends on the conditions each asset operates under.
